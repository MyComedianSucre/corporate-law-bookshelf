## 9.3.2 Public enforcement  
〔ch09-p0075；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_005.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0076"></a>
Public enforcement contrasts with private enforcement insofar as it is initiated by organs of the state (e.g., market regulators or public prosecutors) or private institutions with quasi-governmental powers such as stock exchanges.[124](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1538869) All of our major jurisdictions devote significant resources to public enforcement of securities laws including mandatory disclosure regimes.  
〔ch09-p0076；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_005.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0077"></a>
Two resource-based measures provide a rough indication of the intensity of enforcement by market regulatory authorities in our major jurisdictions.[125](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1539058) Looking at public enforcement staff relative to population, the UK and the U.S. stand out: these two Anglo-Saxon jurisdictions devote at least three times the staff to public securities enforcement (adjusted for population) as any one of our remaining four jurisdictions.[126](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1539624) Enforcement budgets adjusted for jurisdictional GDP yield much the same result. The adjusted enforcement budgets of the UK and U.S. exceed those of France, Germany, and Japan by ratios of three or four.[127](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1539910)  
〔ch09-p0077；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_005.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0078"></a>
As nearly as we can determine, however, the balance between formal and informal public enforcement between the two Anglo-Saxon regimes differs dramatically. U.S. civil and criminal authorities bring many more formal enforcement actions than their UK counterparts, while UK authorities—especially the Financial Services Authority (FSA) and the City—appear to be able to accomplish much more informally, with the proverbial ‘raise of the regulator’s eyebrow’.[128](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1540157)  
〔ch09-p0078；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_005.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0079"></a>
These results do not include many aspects of public enforcement, such as criminal prosecutions and enforcement undertaken by exchanges. But here, too, U.S. formal public enforcement seems more intense, since it imposes ‘real’ prison sentences on top executive or dominant shareholders in high profile cases such as Enron, Worldcom, and Holliger International. Such harsh treatment contrasts with the occasional sentence of ‘at-home confinement’ in equivalent European cases, such as Parmalat.[129](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1540316) Moreover, as discussed in [Chapter 6](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html#filepos429010), higher levels of U.S. private enforcement go hand-in-hand with higher levels of public enforcement.[130](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1540793) Vibrant private litigation should prompt public enforcers to be more active themselves while, conversely, private litigation feeds on evidence gathered by public enforcers.[131](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1540926)  
〔ch09-p0079；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_005.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0080"></a>
On the other hand, the level of U.S. criminal prosecution of issuers and their managers may not differ as markedly as it appears from the analogous level in jurisdictions such as France or Japan, especially in recent years. As discussed in [Chapter 6](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html#filepos429010), managers of French corporations face a significant risk of criminal prosecution should they engage in self-dealing without proper disclosure and approval by shareholders.[132](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1541284) French corporations also face a high risk of criminalliability for misrepresentations.[133](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1541417) For example, in 2007 the AMF seems to have referred about as many misrepresentation-based fraud cases to French public prosecutors as the comparable number of misrepresentation cases referred by the SEC to the U.S. Department of Justice for criminal prosecution during the same period.[134](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1541731) Similarly, the JFSA’s increasing imposition of administrative fines upon Japanese issuers may achieve an enforcement level that may functionally compare with U.S. criminal prosecution activity.
