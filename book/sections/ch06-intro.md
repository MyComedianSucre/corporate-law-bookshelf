## 6 Related-Party Transactions

<a id="ch06-p0003"></a>
In [Chapters 3](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_000.html#filepos193969) and [4](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html#filepos274408), we reviewed the response of company law to agency problems in the context of the ordinary governance of the corporation. [Chapter 5](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html#filepos337430) has shown that all jurisdictions have adopted legal strategies targeting transactions that siphon off assets to the detriment of creditors. Value diversion is, of course, also a core issue in the relationship between managers and shareholders and between controlling and other shareholders.

<a id="ch06-p0004"></a>
This chapter centers upon straightforward techniques for value diversion: related-party transactions. This category includes both transactions in which related parties such as directors and controlling shareholders deal with the corporation—traditional self-dealing and managerial compensation—as well as transactions in which related parties may appropriate value belonging to the corporation—the taking of corporate opportunities and trading in the company’s shares.[1](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1233396)  
〔ch06-p0004；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0005"></a>
In traditional self-dealing, the law’s concern is that an influential manager or a controlling shareholder will transact with the company on terms less favorable than could be obtained in an arm’s length negotiation. Self-dealing typically refers to purchases or sales of assets by related parties, as when a CEO purchases land from her company. But it also refers to other transactions, such as company guarantees in favor of its controlling shareholder and transactions with close relatives of managers or with companies owned by their families. In such cases, the conflicts of interest are acute. Compensation agreements, while technically a form of self-dealing, are unavoidable for companies and thus less suspect. Nevertheless, as hinted at in [Chapter 3](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_000.html#filepos193969), there is an obvious risk of collusion among senior managers and the board in setting compensation levels. For example, directors might approve excessive compensation because they are richly compensated themselves, or because they fear losing their seats on the board if they refuse.[2](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1233618)  
〔ch06-p0005；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0006"></a>
In corporate opportunity cases, related parties take business opportunities that should have been offered to their companies instead.[3](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1234166) Similarly, when trading in the company’s shares on the basis of price-sensitive corporate information (so-called insider trading), officers, directors or controlling shareholders appropriate part of the value of yet undisclosed company information by selling or buying before it is reflected in stock prices.  
〔ch06-p0006；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0007"></a>
Related-party transactions include the routine forms of abusive insider behavior that have come to be dubbed ‘tunneling’ in the economic literature,[4](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1234614) a category that includes all forms of misappropriation of value (assets, cash flows, or the company’s equity itself) by corporate insiders.[5](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1235154) We cover other, more specialized forms of tunneling in later chapters, including misappropriation associated with significant corporate actions in [Chapter 7](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html#filepos505313), misappropriation accompanying control transactions in [Chapter 8](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html#filepos611792), and misappropriation arising from securities fraud other than insider trading in [Chapter 9](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_003.html#filepos741362).
