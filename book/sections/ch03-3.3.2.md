## 3.3.2 Best practices and board structure  
〔ch03-p0039；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch03-p0040"></a>
Japan aside, the canon of best practices in corporate governance is remarkably similar across our jurisdictions, whether it is embodied in hard law or semi-voluntary codes. Jurisdictions differ more in the ‘severity’ of their best practices rather than in the direction of their concerns. One example is international convergence on the standard U.S. triptych of an audit, nomination, and compensation committee within the board, to be staffed—entirely or in part—by independent directors. Here the motivation is clearly to keep control over these crucial functions out of the hands of corporate insiders. As noted above, U.S. listing rules and the Sarbanes-Oxley Act make this board structure mandatory for U.S. listed companies.[74](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1039631) Somewhat less forcefully, the UK’s Combined Code of Corporate Governance recommends that independent directors compose one-half of the membership of the board as a whole, that the board’s chair be independent on appointment, and that the company’s audit committee be entirely independent.[75](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1039858) France, Germany, and Italy follow suit, albeit with less emphasis on independence.[76](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1040222) Thus, with the exception of Japan, the composition and committee structure of the boards in our core jurisdictions appear to be converging.  
〔ch03-p0040；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch03-p0041"></a>
In contrast to directorial independence and committee structure, board size has received less attention in our core jurisdictions, despite its salience in the literature on effective governance.[77](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1041676) No code of best practices limits board size, and only France mandates a maximum size of 18 directors in its company law.[78](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1042282) Nevertheless, as [Table 3–1](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html#filepos228565) illustrates, board size—like committee structure—appears to be converging to the Anglo-American norm of small boards with between nine and twelve directors in all core jurisdictions except Germany.

<a id="ch03-p0043"></a>
To be sure, the numbers in [Table 3–1](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html#filepos228565) require qualification. To begin, the unusually large size of German boards is an artifact of German codetermination law.[80](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1044879) Without this constraint, German companies would undoubtedly have supervisory boards in line with international averages—and indeed several major German companies such as Allianz, BASF, and Porsche have converted into the EU-wide SE form expressly in order to reduce the size of their boards to the SE minimum of 12 directors.[81](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1045175) Similarly, the extent to which ‘independent’ directors are actually independent differs by jurisdiction. French law, for example, still emphasizes the role of the président-directeur général (PDG), a combined chairman of the board and CEO, who typically dominates the board and may even select independent directors with the aid of a controlling shareholder.[82](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1045871) In Italy, also, blockholders often secure their influence by inflating the board with friendly outside directors.[83](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1047330)

<a id="ch03-p0044"></a>
Finally, the puzzle of Japanese boards is not that they average more directors than other jurisdictions, but that they seem to have so few relative to past decades.[84](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1047936) Japanese practice traditionally dictated that boards should be large, chiefly in order to create positions for aging but loyal senior executives.[85](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1048327) But this tradition appears to have changed. [Table 3–1](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html#filepos228565) may exaggerate the magnitude of the change because it is based on the average board size of larger companies listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange, many of which are nonetheless smaller than the same category of companies listed two decades ago (because the number of listed companies has more than doubled during this period). But other factors are also at work. One is that Japanese companies can now adopt a US-style, tripartite committee structure.[86](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1048758) Another is that innovation has also occurred within the traditional Japanese model of the board. Under the so-called Shikkouyakuin system, some Japanese companies now separate strategic decision-making from tactical business decisions by delegating the latter to a new level of corporate officers who might otherwise have served as second-tier directors on a traditional Japanese board.[87](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1049444) This innovation offers the option of smaller boards without altering the number of honorific positions at the company’s apex. (i.e., the sum of directors and executives who serve as shikkou-yakuin).  
〔ch03-p0044；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch03-p0045"></a>
Put differently, in both Germany and Japan, large boards of directors serve organizational functions other than monitoring and managing on behalf of shareholders. This does not mean that traditional Japanese and German companies lack effective governance, however. Arguably, real governance in many German companies occurs within the management board,[88](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1049889) or within supervisory board committees,[89](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1050806) while the supervisory board as a whole functions mainly to disclose management policy to labor representatives and arbitrate potential labor disputes. Similarly, in Japan, de facto decision-making power traditionally resides not in the board as a whole, but in the hands of informal management committees or a single ‘representative’ director, who is also the firm’s CEO.[90](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1051170) The separate board of statutory auditors, which monitors management in collaboration with a company’s outside auditors,[91](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1051331) is generally perceived to be relatively ineffective in Japan, as it is in other jurisdictions that maintain this atrophied form of supervisory board.[92](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1051674)
