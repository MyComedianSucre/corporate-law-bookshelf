## 3.1.4 Facilitating collective action  
〔ch03-p0022；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_000.html)〕

<a id="ch03-p0023"></a>
A final aspect of governance rules is the extent to which they empower dispersed shareholders. Diffuse stock ownership presents shareholders with formidable collective action problems in attempting to exercise their control rights. All of our target jurisdictions address this problem—up to a point.  
〔ch03-p0023；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_000.html)〕

<a id="ch03-p0024"></a>
Voting mechanisms are a conspicuous example. Small shareholders everywhere can exercise their voice at shareholder meetings through one of three mechanisms: mail (or distance) voting, proxy solicitation by corporate partisans, and proxy voting through custodial institutions or other agents. For example, Japanese law allows mid-size and large firms to choose either proxy or mail voting.[37](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1019174) Mail voting requires the distribution of ‘universal ballots’, which permit shareholders to vote on all board nominees in lieu of giving proxies to partisan agents who ultimately vote at shareholders’ meetings. Likewise, France, Germany, and Italy allow corporations to opt for distance voting,[38](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1019638) which also enables shareholders to submit universal ballots.[39](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1019992) By contrast, only the U.S. and the UK rely exclusively on proxy voting among our core jurisdictions.[40](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1020449) In the UK proxy fights are relatively rare, since prior negotiation among institutional investors often resolves conflicts.[41](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1021135) It matters in the U.S., however, where shareholders exercise relatively less influence over managers and the costs to insurgents of nominating candidates and soliciting proxies are high.[42](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1021626)Broadly speaking, all institutions that invest in the market on behalf of multiple beneficiaries can be seen as aggregating control rights, and therefore reducing the collective action problems of disaggregated investors. Indeed, many institutions with financial obligations to their beneficiaries or customers—including pension funds, mutual funds, unit trusts, and insurance companies—have long championed shareholder interests in the UK and the U.S.[43](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1022890) By contrast, purely custodial institutions have no financial interest in the shares they hold, and have traditionally favored company nominees to the extent that they have been empowered to vote custodial shares. For example, U.S. brokerage houses vote their custodial shares in favor of uncontested company nominees,[44](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1023217) while continental European custodians, such as banks and foundations, traditionally played an even stronger, pro-incumbents role in corporate governance.[45](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1023593) In Germany, for example, where supervisory boards could not (or did not) engage in partisan proxy solicitation,[46](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1023884) banks served as depositories for shares, and customarily voted these shares for company nominees by implicit consent.[47](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1024084) After financial pressures[48](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1024630) and legal reform[49](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1024987) largely curbed this practice in the 1990s, voting outcomes in widely held German companies have occasionally become less predictably pro-management.[50](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_009.html#filepos1025687)
