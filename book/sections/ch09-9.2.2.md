## 9.2.2 Quality controls: governance and regulatory strategies  
〔ch09-p0053；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0054"></a>
In addition to mandating disclosure, lawmakers and stock exchanges also impose ‘quality’ restrictions on companies with securities traded on public markets. Indeed, all law that exclusively targets ‘listed’ or ‘public’ companies—i.e., much of the law that we have discussed in earlier chapters—protects public investors by bolstering the quality of publicly-traded corporations. In general terms, the case for these quality controls resembles the case for mandatory disclosure. Unlike the failure of a closely held firm, the failure of a publicly-traded firm harms distant investors and raises capital costs for other companies trading in the same market. Moreover, imposing a more uniform regime on publicly-traded companies increases their transparency, reduces information costs for investors, and may even be viewed as a kind of ‘delegated contracting’.[78](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1515340)

## 9.2.2.1 Governance strategies  
〔ch09-p0055；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0056"></a>
Consider governance strategies first. Most mandatory features of shareholder appointment rights target publicly-traded companies exclusively. These include the hard and soft law that structure shareholder voting rights and nomination procedures, director tenure, the committee structure of boards, the conduct of proxy fights, and disclosure requirements.[79](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1515847) To a lesser extent, stock exchange listing rules mandate direct decision making rights for shareholders, as in the U.S., where the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and NASDAQ mandate shareholder approval of certain equity compensation plans and any transaction that permits companies to issue new shares with voting rights exceeding 20% of shares that are already issued and outstanding.[80](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1516006) And, of course, both hard and soft law mandate extensive use of the trusteeship strategy, in the form of adding independent directors to the board, for publicly-traded companies.[81](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1516427)  
〔ch09-p0056；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0057"></a>
But there is another variation of the trusteeship strategy that is targeted directly at investor protection. Most major jurisdictions maintain at least vestigial screening by regulators or stock exchanges of companies eligible for public trading.  
〔ch09-p0057；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0058"></a>
In the U.S. vernacular, this form of trusteeship strategy is termed ‘merit regulation’. In past decades, many U.S. states permitted state regulators to refuse to approve an issue that failed to conform to certain guidelines, or appeared—to the officials—to be particularly risky without offsetting economic merit. In practice, U.S. state officials rarely bar initial public offerings, although the practice still occurs.[82](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1516555)  
〔ch09-p0058；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0059"></a>
European jurisdictions also engage in merit regulation when they allow regulators to screen applications for exchange listings in the interest of protecting the investing public.[83](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1516965) Thus, the UK’s Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA) authorizes the UK Listing Authority (UKLA) to refuse a listing application that it considers detrimental to the interests of investors.[84](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1517190) Similarly, the French supervisory authority may oppose exchange listings that appear to violate applicable laws, while German stock exchanges are required to withhold listings that fail to comply with provisions enacted to protect the public.[85](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1517375) However, quality-control provisions have recently fallen from favor among regulators.[86](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1517675)  
〔ch09-p0059；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

## 9.2.2.2 Affiliation strategies: entering and exiting public markets  
〔ch09-p0060；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0061"></a>
As we have noted throughout this book, publicly-traded companies are far more heavily regulated than closely held companies. The familiar legal distinction between ‘open’ companies, such as the German Aktiengesellschaft or the French sociétés anonymes, and ‘closed’ companies such as the GmbH and the SARL, arguably serves as a device for subjecting publicly-traded companies to tighter regulatory control. Of course, the overlap between ‘open’ and publicly-traded companies is spotty (not all open companies are publicly traded).[87](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1517843) Nevertheless, a straightforward justification for limiting regulation to ‘open’ companies is that closed companies are, by definition, insulated from public investors and trading markets. In addition, of course, there is a second web of rules specifically geared to trading status which governs publicly-traded companies. These rules, which are addressed in earlier chapters, range from controls on the allocation of voting rights and board structure to regulating tender offers.  
〔ch09-p0061；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0062"></a>
But if much of our earlier discussion implicitly focuses on publicly-traded companies, we have yet to address the listing rules for entering the public market and the delisting and ‘deregistration’ rules for escaping regulation as a public company.  
〔ch09-p0062；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0063"></a>
Listing requirements are designed to exclude presumptively low-quality or illiquid issuers from particular public markets. ‘Official’ or ‘first-tier’ markets typically mandate a minimum size for corporate issuers, a minimum float for listed securities, and a minimum history of published accounts. For example, the NYSE requires a pre-tax corporate income of between $2 million and $4.5 million,[88](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1517988) net tangible assets of $40 million, and a float of publicly-held shares that—depending on market conditions—must exceed $40 million. It also requires a minimum of 1.1 million shares of publicly-held common stock, and a minimum number of shareholders (between 500 and 2,200, depending on trading volume). Other exchanges, with the exception of the Tokyo Stock Exchange,[89](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1518460) impose less rigorous listing requirements. For example, the EC minimum for an exchange listing is €1 million in ‘foreseeable’ market capitalization (or, if this cannot be assessed, in legal capital and reserves), and financial accounts covering the company’s prior operating history for at least three years.[90](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1518816)  
〔ch09-p0063；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0064"></a>
Although the conditions for entering even top-tier markets are modest, the rules governing ‘delisting’ and ‘deregistration’ are less so. Easy exit from publicly-traded and listed company regulation might encourage firms to bait and switch—to attract investors with the implicit promise of full disclosure, and then ‘go dark’ by abandoning their status as public companies. Thus, difficult exit rules serve as a commitment device for publicly-traded firms, and as a protective device for public investors.[91](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1520212)  
〔ch09-p0064；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0065"></a>
Two kinds of legal regimes govern the withdrawal of a company from public reporting obligations in our core jurisdictions. First, all jurisdictions establish objective criteria for allowing companies to cease complying with public reporting obligations. In some jurisdictions, these criteria are quantitative. For example, U.S. securities law ceases to mandate disclosure when the number of a company’s shareholders drops below 300 (or, in some circumstances, 500),[92](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1520574) whereas Japanese public registration requirements cease to apply when the number of an issuer’s shareholders falls below 300 or its legal capital drops below 500 million yen.[93](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1520849) In some jurisdictions that distinguish between the closed and open corporate forms, the choice of the latter form with freely transferable shares triggers disclosure obligations that remain in force until shareholders choose to revert to the closely held form. One example is Germany, where the Aktiengesetz—the corporation code—imposes disclosure obligations aimed at protecting investors in addition to those of the Wertpapierhandelsgesetz, a U.S.-style securities code applicable to listed firms.[94](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1521103) France provides another example of this hybrid approach, combining the disclosure obligations of a continental company law and an Anglo-Saxon-style code of securities regulation.[95](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1522053)  
〔ch09-p0065；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0066"></a>
Second, public companies that are exchange-listed must also comply with the rules governing voluntary delisting.[96](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1522741) For example, the NYSE permits securities to be delisted only upon approval of (1) the company’s audit committee and (2) a majority of the company’s full board of directors; and only after providing shareholders with at least 45 (but no more than 60) days’ written notice.[97](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1523328) In other jurisdictions, the law specifically empowers listing or supervisory authorities to oppose delisting applications when it is in the interests of investors to do so.[98](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1523796) This form of investor protection has, in particular, the advantage of reinforcing the effectiveness of delisting-related company law requirements. For example, German delisting authorities will take into account whether the Aktiengesetz shareholder majority and minority exit rights have been complied with.[99](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1524126)  
〔ch09-p0066；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0067"></a>
Particularly since enactment of the U.S. Sarbanes-Oxley Act, scholars have closely investigated the regulatory costs associated with registration as a publicly-traded company. In the U.S., the concern has been that firms might withdraw from the disclosure regime to avoid these costs.[100](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1524260) While firms have not left the NYSE to list on, say, the London Stock Exchange (LSE) main market,[101](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1524854) many foreign firms cross-listed on U.S. exchanges have delisted or are considering delisting.[102](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1525195) In addition, many small U.S. public companies have gone dark—i.e., ceased to file with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) while continuing to trade on the Over-the-Counter (OTC) market. The relative importance of factors influencing firms’ decisions to go dark is unclear, however. No doubt regulatory costs play an important role.[103](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1526125) But there is also evidence that firms may deregister for other reasons, including poor investment prospects, financial distress, or the desire by their controllers to withhold uncomfortable information from shareholders and regulators.[104](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1527581) Moreover, the costs of complying with SOX seem to be highly variable across companies.[105](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1528565)  
〔ch09-p0067；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_004.html)〕

<a id="ch09-p0068"></a>
By contrast, scholarly concern in our other core jurisdictions—France, Germany, Italy, Japan, and the UK—has centered on the risk that investors may be misled by overly lax disclosure by firms traded on so-called ‘second-tier’ trading systems.[106](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1528882) Firms that can easily shift from the rigorous disclosure required by a first-tier (or ‘official’) listing to the more relaxed requirements of a second-tier (or ‘alternative’) listing, have the power to degrade their own regulatory regimes at will. In effect, shifting from a first-tier to a second-tier listing is going dusky if not entirely dark. The UK’s Financial Services Authority has been particularly aggressive in alerting investors to the significance of the distinction between these two sorts of listings.[107](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1529418) Other European jurisdictions rely on classifying main trading systems as ‘regulated markets’ to signal that these markets mandate high quality disclosure.[108](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1530159)
