## 5.2.2 The rules strategy: legal capital  
〔ch05-p0049；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0050"></a>
If mandatory disclosure helps creditors to protect themselves, then the rules strategy seeks to provide protection for them in a standardized form. The most important rules relate to ‘legal capital’.[80](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1174377) These can apply to at least three separate aspects of corporate finance, which we consider in turn: (1) prescribing a minimum initial investment of equity capital; (2) restrictions on payments out to shareholders; and (3) triggering actions that must be taken following serious depletion of capital.

<a id="ch05-p0051"></a>
## 5.2.2.1 Minimum capital  
〔ch05-p0051；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0052"></a>
The rules governing the minimum investment levels for incorporation are termed ‘minimum capital’ requirements. Amongst our jurisdictions, only those in Europe impose minimum investment thresholds for access to the corporate form. EC law requires public corporations to have initial legal capital of no less than €25,000, whereas member states may set higher thresholds if they wish.[81](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1174962) Although this number is large by Japanese or U.S. standards (which generally require nothing at all),[82](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1175222) it is actually quite small when compared to the actual capital needs of businesses organized as public firms. As a consequence, minimum capital requirements do not appear to create significant barriers to entry to public corporation status.[83](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1175670) As regards non-public companies, most of our jurisdictions nowadays permit incorporation of a non-public company without any minimum capital requirement.[84](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1176298) Only Italy (still) requires a minimum initial capital investment of €10,000.[85](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1177376)  
〔ch05-p0052；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0053"></a>
It seems unlikely that minimum capital requirements provide any real protection to creditors, as a firm’s initial capital is likely to be long gone if it files for bankruptcy. Moreover, the reduction or abolition of minimum capital rules throughout Europe appears to be associated with an increase in entrepreneurship.[86](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1177510) However, most entrepreneurs appear to invest some capital in newly-formed firms, even in the absence of minimum capital rules.[87](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1177889) This may indicate that the presence of capital is a rough-and-ready proxy for the ‘seriousness’ of entrepreneurs, by showing that they commit a non-trivial amount of money to their project.[88](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1178474)  
〔ch05-p0053；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

## 5.2.2.2 Distribution restrictions  
〔ch05-p0054；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0055"></a>
Company laws generally restrict distributions to shareholders—including dividends and share repurchases—in order to prevent asset dilution.[89](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1179167) Although these distribution restrictions vary across jurisdictions, the most common is on the payment of dividends which impair the company’s legal capital—that is, distributions that exceed the difference between the book value of the company’s assets and the amount of its legal capital, as shown in the balance sheet.[90](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1179307)  
〔ch05-p0055；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0056"></a>
Rules restricting distributions can be viewed as an ‘opt-in’ set of standard terms. On this view, any firm that has legal capital in excess of minimum requirements does so by choice, not because of a mandatory requirement.[91](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1179867) In such a situation, distribution constraints simply reinforce the credibility of the shareholders’ promise to retain their capital investment in the firm. From a debtor perspective, such an ‘opt-in’ has the advantage that when there are multiple creditors, transaction costs are low compared to the repeated negotiation of a (possibly diverse) set of contractual covenants. From a creditor perspective, however, legal capital may not offer sufficient protection. Heterogeneity among creditors will result in some of them demanding covenant protection in addition to ‘one size fits all’ legal capital, thus reducing transaction cost savings.[92](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1180860)  
〔ch05-p0056；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0057"></a>
The extent to which distribution restrictions restrict payments to shareholders is affected by the scope of the transactions they cover. In Germany and the UK, the restriction on distributions is applied not only to transactions formally structured as dividends or share repurchases, but also to any undervalue transaction between a company and its shareholders, which the courts may recharacterize as having been a ‘disguised distribution’.[93](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1181046) In the U.S., the efficacy of even the basic distribution restriction is undermined by giving the shareholders, or in some cases the board of directors, power to reduce a company’s legal capital—and hence the level at which the distribution restriction is set—without creditor consent.[94](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1182076) By contrast, Japan and many EU jurisdictions require any reduction in legal capital to be preceded by adequate protection—for example, a third party guarantee—for existing creditors.[95](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1182318)  
〔ch05-p0057；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0058"></a>
The efficacy of distribution restrictions depends upon accounting methodology. Whilst conservative accounting provides less information about the ongoing value of the firms, it is more protective of creditors’ interests than ‘true and fair view’ accounting when it comes to reducing the discretion to pay dividends or otherwise transfer assets to shareholders from the pool that bonds the company’s debts.[96](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1182615) Hence, the increasing reliance on ‘marking-to-market’ in U.S. GAAP may be one of the reasons for the increasing use of profit distribution covenants by U.S. publicly-traded corporations.[97](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1182915) This, in turn, calls into question the continued utility of legal capital restrictions following the use of IFRS accounting methodology by many listed European firms.[98](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1183435)  
〔ch05-p0058；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0059"></a>
A frequent critique of distribution restrictions is that they constrain changes in capital structure by increasing the costs of corporate restructurings such as leveraged buy-outs and the paying off of dissenting shareholders.[99](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1183612) To the extent that corporate finance is bank centered and accounting is conservative, such constraints probably pose little burden on financial transactions. On the other hand, they may be more of a handicap when restructuring firms must switch from bank financing and conservative accounting to capital market financing and ‘true and fair view’ accounting.  
〔ch05-p0059；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

## 5.2.2.3 Loss of capital  
〔ch05-p0060；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0061"></a>
In some jurisdictions—particularly in Europe, but not in the U.S.—there are rules governing actions that must be taken following a serious loss of capital. EC law requires public companies to call a shareholders’ meeting to consider dissolution after a serious loss of capital, defined as net assets falling below half the company’s legal capital.[100](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1184248) Several European jurisdictions have adopted yet stronger rules, mandating those running the firm either to obtain fresh equity finance or stop trading when a certain proportion of legal capital is exhausted.[101](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1184969) In some jurisdictions, such as France and Italy, the company must be put into liquidation should the company’s net assets fall below the statutory minimum capital,[102](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1185149) whereas in others, such as Germany and Switzerland, the company must file for insolvency when value of its net assets has fallen to zero.[103](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1185374)  
〔ch05-p0061；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0062"></a>
In theory, such rules could reinforce the credibility of legal capital as a financial cushion for creditors by acting as capital adequacy provisions similar to those governing financial institutions. Yet given low minimum capital thresholds, even the most stringent loss of capital requirements are concerned more with promoting early filing for bankruptcy than with capital adequacy.[104](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1186032) To be sure, encouraging earlier liquidation or insolvency proceedings will serve to shorten the ‘twilight period’ during which shareholder opportunism can harm creditors. Yet the costs of initiating bankruptcy proceedings may be even higher. Whilst such costs can be avoided by a renegotiation, the more heterogeneous the firm’s creditors, the less likely this will be to succeed.[105](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1186300)  
〔ch05-p0062；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_001.html)〕
