## 6.2.3 Shareholder voting: the decision rights strategy  
〔ch06-p0051；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0052"></a>
As an alternative or complement to disinterested board approval of related-party transactions, jurisdictions may require or encourage shareholder approval. Shareholders, after all, are the parties who lose from managerial or controlling shareholder opportunism. Outside directors are disinterested, while shareholders are affirmatively interested in preserving corporate value. It might therefore appear that the shareholders meeting should screen conflicted transactions. But, of course, this reasoning runs counter to the logic of the corporate form, which fosters delegation of decision-making to the board in order to avoid the costs and the collective action problems associated with direct governance by shareholders.  
〔ch06-p0052；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0053"></a>
No jurisdiction mandates across-the-board shareholder approval for related-party transactions, not even with controlling shareholders. This is because doing so might be excessively cumbersome (especially for companies that are integrated into groups, where such transactions can be very frequent) and could also raise conflicted voting issues.[81](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1266849) In addition, most of our sample jurisdictions are reluctant to mandate minority shareholder approval, perhaps out of concern for the control rights of shareholder majorities. Indeed, ‘majority of the minority’ voting is a well-established institution in the U.S. and UK,[82](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1267004) two jurisdictions in which large companies typically lack a controlling shareholder, but is much less developed in continental Europe, where controlling shareholders have significant voting (and lobbying) power.  
〔ch06-p0053；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0054"></a>
There is greater convergence among our jurisdictions in the selected related-party transactions that require shareholder approval.[83](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1267158) Nowadays, all of our jurisdictions require listed firms to submit some forms of director and senior officer compensation to shareholder approval.[84](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1267705) This is a direct consequence of considerable increases in executive remuneration levels around the world and the controversies they generated.[85](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1268261) U.S. stock exchanges require a shareholder vote on all equity compensation plans whereas some states mandate shareholder approval of stock option plans.[86](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1268876) In the EU, where the European Commission has recommended that member states adopt legislation aimed at giving shareholders in listed companies a say regarding director and senior officer remuneration, most member states have adopted rules on prior shareholder approval of share-based incentive schemes (Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, and the UK being the most demanding by mandating such a vote without limitations).[87](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1269371) While shareholders generally vote on the remuneration policy for supervisory board members, member states have been much less keen to give shareholders a specific say about the remuneration criteria for, or individual compensation of, one-tier board members or senior executives.[88](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1269782)  
〔ch06-p0054；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0055"></a>
Beyond that, France and the UK appear to be most inclined to give shareholders a say on related-party transactions. French statutory law requires shareholder ratification of all non-routine self-dealing transactions entered into during the prior financial year.[89](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1270433) The UK mandates ex ante shareholder approval for non-routine transactions with directors and large shareholders of listed companies.[90](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1270773) For other companies, the Companies Act requires shareholder approval of some transactions with directors, in particular substantial property transactions and credit transactions.[91](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1270921)  
〔ch06-p0055；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0056"></a>
While the French approach seems very encompassing, in practice, it may not have a substantially larger scope than UK law. As in the UK for listed companies, it leaves corporate decision-makers with some discretion in deciding whether a transaction is non-routine and, thus, needs to be approved by shareholders.[92](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1271053) In contrast with France, further, the UK shares with the U.S. a judicial tradition of accepting minority shareholder approval as the most reliable method of screening conflicted transactions with controlling shareholders.[93](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1271284)  
〔ch06-p0056；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0057"></a>
Other jurisdictions are less insistent on shareholder approval. German law requires minority approval in non-publicly-traded corporations, but only when the articles of association or the firm’s management submit a related-party transaction to shareholder vote.[94](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1271731) Italy, for its part, requires shareholder approval when a director of a publicly-traded corporation wants to sit on the board of a competing corporation.[95](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1272063)  
〔ch06-p0057；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch06-p0058"></a>
It is worth noting (and somewhat surprising) that the timing of shareholder consent is not an important consideration anywhere. Shareholder approval appears to have the same legal effect, whether it precedes or follows a conflicted transaction, although intuitively ex ante approval might seem to be more effective in screening self-dealing transactions. Indeed, shareholder ratification shifts the burden of proof in U.S. courts not only after self-dealing occurs, but also after it is discovered and challenged in court. Even France, a jurisdiction that particularly values shareholder consent, allows ex post validation of conflicted transactions that required ex ante board authorization by law but were never actually submitted to the board.[96](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1272206) The UK goes further still by permitting post-transaction ratification by shareholders to protect directors against most liability claims and, if ratification takes place ‘within a reasonable period’, to validate their substantial property transactions and loans.[97](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_011.html#filepos1272429)
