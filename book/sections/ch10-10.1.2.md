## 10.1.2 The controlling-minority shareholders conflict  
〔ch10-p0017；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_006.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0018"></a>
As with the conflict between managers and shareholders as a class, the distinction between controlled and dispersed jurisdictions leads to a priori expectations about how corporate law might intervene in conflicts between controlling and minority shareholders. We would naturally expect controlled jurisdictions to police related-party transactions lightly, since these are among the principal channels through which controlling shareholders can extract private benefits from their firms. Conversely, jurisdictions in which widely-held firms dominate might be expected to police self-dealing more closely, not only because their top managers generally lack the means to engage in tunneling (management compensation aside), but also because increasing the private benefits available to controlling shareholders undermines the stability of board-centered governance by encouraging investors to seek control blocks.  
〔ch10-p0018；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_006.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0019"></a>
By and large, the historical legal differences among our core jurisdictions seem to confirm these expectations. U.S. corporate law and securities regulation have vigorously policed self-dealing transactions with controlling shareholders since the end of the 1970s if not before. The UK City Code originated a tough mandatory bid rule, which ensured that minority shareholders would participate pro rata in any premia following the aggregation or sale of corporate control. And Japan has long subscribed to a strong equal-treatment norm, which limits the opportunities for controlling shareholders to exploit minority shareholders. However, here again, only the U.S. closely fits the stereotype of a self-consciously board-centric jurisdiction, with the UK and Japan being better described as shareholder centric. The UK’s mandatory bid rule essentially reflects the interests and influence of institutional investors holding large but non-controlling stakes, whereas the Japanese equal treatment norm caters to the friendly coalitions of shareholders upon which managers rely to secure their grip on the firm.  
〔ch10-p0019；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_006.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0020"></a>
By contrast, France, Germany, and Italy have traditionally been less keen to police related-party transactions with controlling shareholders. Even in Germany, which is well-known for its regulation of corporate groups through Konzernrecht, the effectiveness of minority protection against tunneling is debatable. The same is true for France, even though it relies primarily on criminal prosecution—rather than civil actions by minority shareholders—to deter opportunism by controlling shareholders. While criminal prosecution may be a powerful deterrent in some circumstances, it is inherently limited to only the most flagrant cases of opportunism by controlling shareholders.  
〔ch10-p0020；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_006.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0021"></a>
On the other hand, the match between ownership structure and mandatory disclosure has been weakened significantly over the past decade. We conjectured that mandatory disclosure and shareholder-friendly accounting methodologies were consistent with—and perhaps even essential for—a dispersed jurisdiction, while a controlled jurisdiction would oppose them. Historically, this prediction was on the mark. Mandatory disclosure regimes developed earlier and more rapidly in the U.S., UK, and Japan than they did in continental Europe. The same is true for ‘fair value’ accounting methodologies. Today, however, the disclosure gap between our controlled and dispersed jurisdictions has narrowed. The U.S. retains an edge in the scope and enforcement of disclosure obligations, but the global accounting methodologies in use today—IFRS and U.S. GAAP—both derive from an Anglo-Saxon tradition of shareholder friendliness.  
〔ch10-p0021；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_006.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0022"></a>
What weakened the link between ownership structure and disclosure law? We suspect that the rise of domestic and global equity markets made expanded disclosure attractive, even to controlling shareholders. While disclosure has constrained their ability to extract private benefits from firms, it has also enhanced the liquidity of their holdings and lowered the cost of attracting additional (minority) equity capital for their firms.  
〔ch10-p0022；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_006.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0023"></a>
The match between ownership structure and minority protection has been weakened much like the link between ownership structure and mandatory disclosure. Italy now mandates the right of minority shareholders to elect a director to the board of listed firms. In Germany, minority shareholders have been able to make increasingly aggressive use of their right to challenge shareholder resolutions that appear to harm minority shareholders.  
〔ch10-p0023；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_006.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0024"></a>
A similar story may explain the widespread embrace of governance reforms by controlled jurisdictions. We conjectured earlier that independent directors with limited time budgets pose little threat to entrenched managers while possibly legitimizing them. Do we expect independent directors to fulfill a similar function for controlled companies? They can be dismissed at the whim of the controlling shareholder. But personal liability can inspire even independent directors to rise against abusive controlling shareholders. Thus, controlled jurisdictions are no more likely than dispersed jurisdictions to resist the imposition of independent directors. The gain is a better price for publicly-traded minority shares and, therefore, a reduction in the cost of capital; the loss is the possible exit of public markets by firms controlled by abusive shareholders, a result that is likely to be supported by the great majority of controlling shareholders. Germany and Japan, which seem to have at least partially rejected the current orthodoxy of good corporate governance are really exceptions. Independent directors play a lesser role in these jurisdictions mainly because these jurisdictions approach the shareholder-non shareholder agency problem differently—through codetermination in Germany and by continuing to allow boards dominated by managers in Japan.
