## 5.4.3 Managerial incentives  
〔ch05-p0116；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0117"></a>
The standard employed for directorial liability to creditors has the highest intensity in Japan and the lowest in the U.S., continental Europe being closest to the Japanese regime and the UK to the U.S. regime.  
〔ch05-p0117；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0118"></a>
In Japan, creditors have standing to sue directors even if the company is solvent, in particular if they fail to properly monitor managers. Closely held companies are the primary target here, reflecting the concentrated ownership and debt structure of that category of firms. Banks have, obviously, an interest in getting managerial incentives aligned with their interests in the vicinity of insolvency. Controlling shareholders, for their part, have no strong reasons to oppose such an outcome as it does not interfere with contractual arrangements or the use of private workouts.  
〔ch05-p0118；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0119"></a>
The U.S. is at the opposite pole, with directorial liability to creditors having the lowest intensity amongst our jurisdictions. There is a shift in the content of directors’ duty of loyalty in relation to insolvent firms, but its practical impact remains limited.[200](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1232762) Once more, this reflects both widely dispersed ownership in large public firms and a fragmented banking industry. Managers have no interest in high intensity liability and creditor heterogeneity makes it unattractive for banks to lobby for a more stringent approach.  
〔ch05-p0119；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_002.html)〕

<a id="ch05-p0120"></a>
In France, Germany, and Italy, the approach is similar to the one adopted in Japan, albeit creditors do not have standing to sue directors of solvent companies. Directors of financially distressed firms can be held liable simply for failing to take action following serious loss of capital, an across-the-board outcome which is also consistent with the generally more concentrated ownership and debt structure of large continental European firms. In the UK, on the other hand, there is a U.S.-like shift in the content of the duty of loyalty for directors of insolvent firms. In addition, however, the UK imposes a negligence-based liability on directors for ‘wrongful trading’, albeit this duty is usually only triggered when the firm is cash-flow insolvent.[201](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_010.html#filepos1232896) This approach is, again, in line with the less dispersed ownership and more concentrated debt structure in the UK’s large public firms.
