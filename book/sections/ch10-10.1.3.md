## 10.1.3 The shareholders-non shareholders conflict  
〔ch10-p0025；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_007.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0026"></a>
Finally, consider the most plausible connection between ownership structure and the protection of non-shareholder constituencies—assuming, as before, that corporate law mirrors the interests of either managers or controlling shareholders. One might naturally suppose that the latter are more likely than managers to behave opportunistically towards other stakeholders. In contrast to shareholder interests, managerial incentives often coincide with those of non-shareholder constituencies. Managers, creditors, employees, and the state all share an interest in safeguarding the firm’s ability to meet its fixed obligations—interest, wages, and taxes—but none of these parties benefits directly from firm revenue beyond what is needed to meet fixed obligations.  
〔ch10-p0026；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_007.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0027"></a>
Thus, controlled jurisdictions should generally offer only limited protection to stakeholders, just as they offer limited protection to minority shareholders. By contrast, stakeholder protection should be more developed in dispersed jurisdictions. How does this prediction compare to our finding in previous chapters?  
〔ch10-p0027；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_007.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0028"></a>
As with other corporate agency problems, the answer is mixed. Mandatory creditor protection is strong in continental European jurisdictions, Japan, and the UK—especially if insolvency law is included. It is arguably weaker in the U.S., insofar as U.S. law provides little ex ante protection for corporate creditors, minimal director liability toward creditors, and a debtor-in-possession bankruptcy regime.  
〔ch10-p0028；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_007.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0029"></a>
Two controlled jurisdictions—Germany and, to a lower degree, France—provide for employee participation on boards of directors. Codetermination and a two-tier board structure make the selection of independent directors to the board unusually sensitive in Germany, where the law presumes that supervisory board members are either shareholder representatives or labor representatives. Independent directors are neither, which explains the resistance to corporate governance reforms aiming at fostering their role, since the defection of any shareholder or labor director would transform the balance of power in the boardrooms of large German firms. None of our dispersed-shareholder jurisdictions directly provides for employee participation, but one cannot fail to notice that an overwhelming majority of listed Japanese companies have not opted to appoint a significant number of independent directors as they are encouraged to do by recent reforms. Instead, they prefer to keep the traditional Japanese board structure in which past or present corporate officers constitute a large majority of directors.  
〔ch10-p0029；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_007.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0030"></a>
And if one steps outside the domain of corporate law, it is clear that non-shareholder constituencies (other than creditors) receive far more legal and political protection in controlled than in dispersed jurisdictions. Germany, France, and Italy, for example, strongly regulate terms of employment contracts in the labor market, and their governments also hold large and influential shareholdings in many of their countries’ largest companies.  
〔ch10-p0030；[EPUB 原文](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_007.html)〕

<a id="ch10-p0031"></a>
Clearly, the naïve expectation that corporate law—or law generally—will minimize the protection accorded to non-shareholder constituencies in controlled jurisdictions is wrong. Non-shareholder constituencies do not resemble minority shareholders. As others have pointed out, the most likely reason is that employee and state interests in our controlled jurisdictions carry more political punch than the interests of minority shareholders do—and perhaps more punch than even the interest of controlling shareholders carry.[7](../epub_source/The_Anatomy_-_Corporate_Law_split_012.html#filepos1554197)
